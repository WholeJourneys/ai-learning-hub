Using Claude effectively

Pick the right model, spend less of your usage, and choose the right app for the job. You choose the model and the effort, and you can change either at any point in a chat.

1. Match the model to the job

Pick the lowest setting that looks right. If the answer is thin or loses the thread, move up one step and carry on in the same chat.

HaikuFastest, lightestQuick questions, reformatting, short rewrites, simple lookups. Haiku 5.5 (7 October) is the first Haiku with an effort setting, default medium. check your picker
SonnetStart hereEveryday work: drafting emails and posts, summaries, pulling data from exports, building simple documents and graphics. Medium effort.
OpusHeavier liftingMessy multi-step jobs: matching several sources, browser-based tasks, long analysis, debugging something that keeps breaking. High effort.
FableTop tierRarely needed. The most capable tier, above Opus, for genuinely hard problems where Opus on High has already fallen short. Availability depends on your plan.
Effort is a separate dial from the model. Extra high and Max are effort settings, not models. Keep them for problems a lighter setting has already failed.

2. Habits that save more than the model choice

1. Start a new chat for each new job.

Long chats cost more per message. Carry over only what the next job needs (see section 8).

2. Give Claude files instead of making it browse.

An export or attachment is faster and far cheaper than clicking through a website.

3. Paste the exact link or file up front.

Naming the source saves a round of searching.

4. Optional: switch off tools you will not use.

Mainly for long, heavy sessions. See section 5 for what to keep on.

5. Ask Claude to flag the setting.

Use the standing instruction below so it tells you when a lighter or stronger setup fits.

Standing instruction to copy. Paste it, then say “Save that as a preference.”

At the start of each task, tell me in one line if a lighter model or lower effort would handle it, or if it needs a stronger one.

3. Desktop app or web: which to open

Rule of thumb: if the work lives in the conversation or in your connected apps, use web. If it lives in files or windows on your computer, use desktop.

Web or phone

  • Quick questions, brainstorming and writing on the go
  • Research with web search, summaries and drafts
  • Building shareable pages, documents and graphics
  • Connected apps such as email, calendar and cloud storage
  • Picking a chat back up from another device
  • Longer tasks you can leave running: Cowork now works in every conversation and on web and mobile (rolling out gradually) check your picker

Desktop app

  • Reading and saving files in folders on your computer
  • Working in websites where you are already signed in, through the browser tools
  • Operating apps on your screen when there is no other route in
  • Anything that touches many local files at once

5. Connectors and web search: what to keep on

Optional housekeeping. Leaving everything on works fine for most days. Pruning matters most in long, heavy sessions.

Keep on day to day

  • Gmail and Google Calendar. Drafting client replies, checking dates and availability.
  • Google Drive and Dropbox. Itineraries, photo archives and source documents.
  • Web search. Anything current: prices, schedules, opening times, rules.

Turn on only when needed

  • Airtable. Operator and supplier lists, data matching, imports.
  • Supabase. Only when working on a database-backed tool.
  • Replit and Lovable. Only while building or checking a site or app.
  • Canva. Only while designing graphics, flyers or social posts.
  • Todoist. When you want tasks read, added or updated.
  • Web search off for pure writing and editing jobs.

6. Two dials: model and effort

They do different jobs.

Model = how capable

Which Claude you use. A bigger model copes with harder, messier, longer jobs. Like choosing which planner to hire.

Effort = how long it thinks

How long it thinks before it answers. More effort means more care and checking, but slower replies and more of your usage. Like how much time that planner spends on your request.

7. Which dial to turn: climb one step at a time

Go up only as far as you need, in the same chat. You can step back down at any time.

Step 1

Sonnet + Medium. Start here for most work.

Step 2

Sonnet + High. Raise the effort when the answer is the right kind but sloppy, shallow or missed details.

Step 3

Opus + High. Switch the model when it loses the thread, the job is big or tangled, or Step 2 is still off.

Step 4

Opus + Extra high / Max. Rarely, only when High has genuinely fallen short.

Know it is a big job from the start? Skip straight to Step 3. Simple job and the answer is slow or overkill? Lower the effort first, then drop to a smaller model.

In one line: effort is about care, the model is about capability. Sloppy answer? Raise effort. Job too big for it? Switch the model.

8. When to start a new chat

Time to move on when…

  • Replies slow down, or your usage drains faster than it should
  • Claude forgets earlier details, repeats itself or contradicts itself
  • The job is finished and you are starting a different one
  • The chat is full of dead ends and corrections

Stay in the same chat when…

  • You are still refining the same piece of work
  • Claude needs to keep lots of detail from earlier in the chat
  • It is a short follow-up or a quick fix
  • The chat is still working well and answers stay on track

9. Carry it over in four steps

1. Ask for a handoff note before you leave.

Paste this into the long chat and let Claude write it:

2. Save the real files.

A note carries words, not attachments. Download PDFs, images and documents, or save them to Drive or Dropbox.

3. Start the new chat and paste the note first.

Re-attach only the files it needs, then say: “Continue from this note.”

4. Name it right away, and keep the old chat.

The old chat is your backup. Do not delete it until the new one is working well.

Write a handoff note so I can continue in a new chat. Include: the goal, decisions made, final wording or versions, file names, what is finished, what is left to do, and any rules to follow. Keep it to one page.

For things you use again and again (brand notes, templates, style guides), put them in a Project so every chat inside it starts with them. Memory can help recall earlier work (Settings, then Memory; entries are now editable under Topics check your picker), but a handoff note is the safest way to carry the details of one job.

10. Name chats so you can find them

Subject - Task - Part number - Month Year. Put the main subject first so related chats sit together.

ChatRegional Guide - Map Draft - Part 2 - Oct 2026
ChatClient Emails - Follow-ups - Oct 2026

Add “Part 2” when you continue a job in a new chat. Add DONE to finished jobs. Use words you would search for. To rename on the web, open the chat, click its name at the top and choose Rename; in the apps look for Rename in the chat menu.

11. Worked example: an itinerary chat

Sample scenario (fictional): a designer is laying out a complicated 9-day walking itinerary with three bases, optional days and ferry transfers. The chat has run long. She wants to keep every decision and the reasons behind them, and reuse the approach on future itineraries.

A handoff note must carry six things. Ask for them by name: decisions and why, rejected options and why, constraints, layout and style rules, current status, open questions.

12. Step 1: hand off at a milestone, not when the chat is full

Ask early. A summary from a chat that is already confused will be confused too. Milestones: layout agreed, days 1 to 5 drafted, final review.

Write a handoff note so I can continue in a new chat. Include: the goal; layout decisions and WHY; options we rejected and WHY; constraints; style and wording rules; status of each day; open questions; and the next step. Then tell me what you left out.

Sample note it produces (fictional)

HANDOFF  Coastal Walk, 9 days, Part 1 to 2
GOAL     Two-column day layout: schedule left, notes right
DECIDED  3 bases, so luggage moves twice (fewer transfers). Max 6 hrs walking a day (client pace)
REJECTED Daily hotel changes (too many transfers). Single column (too long to scan)
CONSTRAINTS Arrive Saturday only. Ferry runs Tue to Sun
STATUS   Days 1-5 drafted, days 6-9 outlined
OPEN     Rest day: day 4 or day 6?
RULES    Plain language. 24h times. End each day with a "tonight" line
NEXT     Draft days 6-9 in the same layout

Read the note and correct it before you leave. It is your record.

13. Step 2: turn the thinking into a playbook

Leave out client details. Then reuse it on every new itinerary.

From this chat, write a reusable itinerary layout playbook: the layout structure, pacing rules, how we handle options and transfers, wording style, and a checklist to run before sending. Explain the reason behind each rule.

1. Save the playbook as a file and keep it in a Project, Drive or Dropbox.

2. Start a new chat and attach the playbook.

3. Say: “Use this playbook to build an itinerary for [trip]. Ask me what is missing first.”

4. At the end, ask Claude to suggest updates to the playbook. Keep the name and raise the version.

How this page stays current

Model names, plan limits and settings change fast, so this page is re-checked against the vendor's official notes every two weeks. Each pass looks for four things, in this order:

  1. A setting or feature this page teaches being renamed, moved or retired.
  2. Plan, price and limit changes.
  3. New models or levels in the picker.
  4. New abilities that save a small operator real time.
